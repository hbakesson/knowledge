Engineering Director — VP Interview

Leadership framework
& interview preparation

A practical reference covering decision-making, prioritisation, change leadership, and execution — with real-world Q&A prep for your VP conversation.

Saying yes & no

Say yes
Aligns with strategy
The request moves the team or org toward a stated goal. Clear call — commit and resource it properly.
Say yes
You own it
It's in your remit, resources exist, and the cost of inaction is higher than the cost of doing it.
Say yes
Reversible decision
Low cost to undo. Bias toward action — learn fast, correct course. Don't over-deliberate.
Say no
Competes with P0
Every yes is a no to something else. Guard the top priority ruthlessly, even when the ask is reasonable.
Say no
Wrong owner
It's important but belongs to another team. Redirect clearly — don't absorb work that isn't yours to own.
Say no
Irreversible & unclear
High stakes, low signal. Slow down, gather data, and decide when you have enough information.
Real world: When a stakeholder asks you to take on a new initiative mid-quarter, ask: "Which of our current P0s does this replace?" That question alone surfaces the real trade-off and puts the decision back where it belongs.

Competing priorities

1
Customer impact
External beats internal, always
2
Strategic alignment
Tied to an OKR? Move it up the list
3
Time sensitivity
Deadline vs. urgency — not the same thing
4
Effort vs. return
Quick win, or long game? Both are valid
5
Cost of waiting
What breaks if we delay by one sprint?
When everything is urgent: Ask each stakeholder to rank their requests against each other, not against your list. People reprioritise quickly when they're ranking their own asks.

STAR model

S
Situation
What was the context? Set the scene in two sentences. Don't over-explain.
T
Task
What was your specific responsibility? Make your role clear — not "we", but what you owned.
A
Action
What did you do? This is where most time should go. Be specific on how, not just what.
R
Result
What was the measurable outcome? Numbers, timelines, team impact. Include what you'd do differently.
Use STAR upward too. When communicating a decision to a VP, structure it as: here's the situation, here's what I was tasked with, here's what I did and why, here's the result so far. It builds trust and makes you easy to report on.

Driving change

Mindset
Start with the why
People resist change they don't understand. Lead with the problem, not the solution. Let them feel the pain first.
Tactic
Find early adopters
One visible win from a trusted peer beats ten slide decks from leadership. Find your champion on the team.
Tactic
Reduce friction in the new way
Make the desired behaviour the path of least resistance. Default it in. Remove the steps that slow adoption.
Pitfall
Don't announce — embed
Change that lives in a kickoff meeting dies in a kickoff meeting. Embed it in rituals, retros, and reviews.

Execution — key initiatives

Focus
Three priorities max
If everything is a key initiative, nothing is. Pick three, protect them, and be explicit about what didn't make the list.
Cadence
Weekly check-in rhythm
Short, structured. Status → blockers → decisions needed. Not a report to leadership — a conversation with the team.
Clarity
Owner + deadline + done
Every initiative needs all three. Missing any one and it will drift. "Definition of done" is the most skipped step.
Key initiative anti-pattern: A list of eight "priorities" with no single owner and no agreed definition of done. Ask your team to name the one thing they're accountable for this quarter. If they can't, that's the first thing to fix.

Interview Q&A — VP round

Your answer framework
Start by describing how you connect OKRs to engineering roadmap decisions. Explain your process for translating a business goal into a technical priority — ideally with a concrete example. Mention how you communicate this downward to the team and upward to leadership.
Strong signal: "I start every quarter by asking which three customer outcomes matter most to the business, then work backwards to what the team needs to ship to enable them. I also make explicit what we're not doing and why."
Your answer framework
Use STAR. Be honest that the person was valuable and that the situation required nuance. Show that you separated impact from behaviour, had a direct 1:1 conversation about the specific behaviour, and gave clear expectations with a timeline. Describe the outcome — whether they changed, or what happened next if they didn't.
What VPs listen for: That you didn't avoid the conversation, that you were direct but fair, and that you protected the team from the collateral damage. Avoiding conflict is a red flag at director level.
Your answer framework
Describe a structured approach: first diagnose whether it's a scope, capacity, or clarity problem — they require different interventions. Then explain how you communicate the delay upward early (bad news doesn't age well), what you do to reset scope or timeline, and how you prevent the same pattern from repeating.
Strong signal: "I'd rather tell a VP we're three weeks late in week two than in week ten. Early transparency gives us options; late disclosure just gives us apologies."
Your answer framework
Show that you distinguish between reversible and irreversible decisions — reversible ones you make quickly with the data you have; irreversible ones you slow down on. Describe how you identify the minimum viable signal needed to proceed, and how you time-box the decision process so analysis doesn't become paralysis.
Frame to use: "Most decisions at director level are two-way doors — I can walk back through if I'm wrong. For those, I'd rather make a call and learn than wait for certainty that won't come."
Your answer framework
Talk about how you identify potential early, give stretch assignments deliberately, and create space to fail safely. Mention how you give feedback — regularly, specifically, and in both directions. If you can name someone you've developed who has moved into a leadership role, that's the strongest signal you can give.
What separates directors from senior engineers: "My job is to make myself unnecessary for the day-to-day. If the team can't operate without me for two weeks, I haven't built the right structure."
Your answer framework
Use STAR. Be honest about the resistance — don't minimise it. Show that you diagnosed where it was coming from (fear, loss of ownership, genuine disagreement), adapted your approach, and built enough coalition to move forward. Describe what you'd do differently.
What VPs look for: That you treated resistance as signal, not noise. Leaders who steamroll resistance tend to generate silent sabotage. Leaders who listen tend to build better solutions.
Your answer framework
Describe your prioritisation framework (customer impact, strategic alignment, effort vs. return). Explain how you bring visibility to the trade-offs rather than absorbing them quietly. Show that you escalate decisions about priority when needed, rather than trying to satisfy everyone by doing everything slowly.
Powerful line: "I try to make the trade-off visible to the stakeholders themselves. When they see their request next to the P0 it would displace, the conversation changes quickly."
Your answer framework
Pick something real, significant enough to be credible, but not so catastrophic it defines you. Show three things: what happened, what you did to fix it, and what changed in how you lead as a result. The learning matters more than the mistake itself.
What kills credibility: A "mistake" that is secretly a humble-brag ("I worked too hard") or something so minor it signals you haven't really been tested. VPs want leaders who've felt real failure and grown from it.
Your answer framework
Show that you distinguish between disagreeing with the what (push back with data, once, clearly) vs. disagreeing with the how (more room to adapt and own it). Describe how you voice disagreement constructively — privately before publicly, with specific reasoning and an alternative, not just objection. And describe how you commit fully once a decision is made.
The principle: "Disagree and commit. I'll push back once, clearly and with evidence. If the decision goes the other way, I execute it as if it were my own idea — because the team deserves a leader who's all in, not one who hedges."
Your answer framework
Talk about creating short-term wins within a long arc, being honest about uncertainty rather than papering over it, and connecting daily work to a mission that matters. Mention how you monitor energy levels — not just velocity — and what you do when you see the team burning out.
Honest framing: "I don't try to pretend everything is fine when it isn't. The team can tell. What I can do is remind them why the hard work matters, celebrate the real milestones, and remove the obstacles that make it feel pointless."

Questions to ask the VP

On the role itself
"What does success look like for this role in the first six months — and how will you know?"
On the team
"What's the thing the team is best at today, and what's the thing you most want to change?"
On leadership style
"How do you prefer to receive bad news — and what's the best way to escalate a decision that needs your input?"
On strategy
"Which of the engineering org's current priorities do you feel most uncertain about, and why?"
On culture
"What kind of leader has thrived here, and what kind hasn't?"
On growth
"What's the biggest opportunity you see for this team in the next year that isn't yet on the roadmap?"
One rule: Don't ask questions you could have answered by reading the company website. Good questions signal you've already been thinking like someone in the role.
