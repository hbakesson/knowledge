# Accelerate: Summary & Engineering Manager Investigation Playbook

---

## Part I: What We Found

### Chapter 1: Accelerate (Introduction)
* **Summary:** The chapter introduces the four-year research program behind the book. It highlights that technology is a key differentiator for business growth, but traditional maturity models (which assume a static, lock-step end state) are flawed. Instead, organizations must focus on a **capabilities model**—a dynamic, multi-dimensional framework for continuous improvement based on 24 key capabilities.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Maturity vs. Capabilities:** Are we treating our DevOps journey as a "project to be finished" (maturity mindset) or as a continuous improvement loop (capabilities mindset)?
  * [ ] **Maturity vanity metrics:** Are we measuring tool adoption (e.g., "we use Kubernetes, therefore we are mature") instead of outcomes (e.g., "how fast can we deliver value safely")?

---

### Chapter 2: Measuring Performance
* **Summary:** Demonstrates that software delivery performance can be measured rigorously using four key metrics (Deployment Frequency, Lead Time for Changes, Time to Restore Service, and Change Fail Rate). Crucially, the data proves there is **no trade-off between speed and stability**—high-performing teams excel at both, while low-performing teams struggle with both.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Delivery Metrics Baseline:** What are our team's current baselines for:
    * *Deployment Frequency:* Do we deploy multiple times per day, weekly, or only monthly/quarterly?
    * *Lead Time for Changes:* How many days/weeks does a commit take to reach production?
    * *Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR):* In an outage, how long does it take to restore service?
    * *Change Fail Rate:* What percentage of production deploys require immediate remediation/hotfixes?
  * [ ] **Metrics Visibility:** Do we have automated dashboards showing these four metrics to the team, or are they calculated manually/hidden?

---

### Chapter 3: Measuring and Changing Culture
* **Summary:** Leverages sociologist Ron Westrum’s typology of organizational culture to classify environments into three categories: Pathological (power-oriented), Bureaucratic (rule-oriented), and Generative (performance-oriented). The research proves that a **Generative culture** predicts higher software delivery performance and lower burnout. Crucially, the authors show you can *act* your way into a better culture by adopting technical and Lean management practices.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Culture Type Assessment:** Does our team operate in a Generative structure (high cooperation, shared risks, blameless post-mortems, novelty welcomed) or a Bureaucratic/Pathological one (silos, blame, responsibilities avoided, failure punished)?
  * [ ] **Information Flow:** When problems or anomalies occur, is the team comfortable surfacing them immediately, or is there a tendency to hide mistakes out of fear?
  * [ ] **Post-Mortem Hygiene:** Are our post-mortems truly blameless, focusing on systemic fixes, or do we subtly look for individuals to blame?

---

### Chapter 4: Technical Practices
* **Summary:** Explores the technical practices that predict continuous delivery: comprehensive version control (including configuration and environment code), automated testing, automated deployment, continuous integration (CI), and trunk-based development. These practices directly decrease deployment pain and team burnout.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Trunk-Based Development:** Do developers merge their code to the main trunk daily, or do we have feature branches that live for days/weeks?
  * [ ] **Automated Testing Reliability:** Are our automated tests fast, reliable, and run on every commit? Do developers trust them, or do they bypass them because of flakiness?
  * [ ] **Deployment Pain Audit:** Ask your team directly: *"On a scale of 1-10, how painful are our deployments?"* If deployments require out-of-hours shifts, manual checklists, or high anxiety, investigate what specific steps can be automated.
  * [ ] **Version Control Coverage:** Is *everything* needed to reproduce our environments (IaC, DB migration scripts, pipeline configurations) in version control?

---

### Chapter 5: Architecture
* **Summary:** The research shows that the specific technology stack used (legacy, microservices, mainframe, or cloud) does not predict delivery performance. Instead, what matters is **architectural loose coupling**—specifically, whether teams can test, deploy, and change their services independently of other teams and systems.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Independent Deployability:** Can our team deploy our services to production on demand, without coordinating releases with other services or teams?
  * [ ] **Independent Testability:** Can we run tests in isolation, or do we require a shared, integrated environment (where other teams' changes can block us)?
  * [ ] **Conway’s Law Alignment:** Are our software boundaries aligned with our team boundaries (so a single team owns a cohesive system block), or do our developers constantly have to make changes in codebases owned by other teams?

---

### Chapter 6: Integrating Infosec into the Delivery Lifecycle
* **Summary:** Shifting security left—integrating security checks, automated scanning, and pre-approved security libraries into the daily development and deployment pipeline—speeds up delivery and increases stability. Gating security at the very end of the lifecycle slows teams down and leads to bypassed controls.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Shift-Left Security:** Are security checks automated within our CI/CD pipeline (e.g., dependency vulnerability scanning, static analysis), or is security a manual review done at the end of the project?
  * [ ] **SecOps Collaboration:** Do security practitioners work *with* developers to build pre-approved libraries and templates, or do they act strictly as a gatekeeper/external audit board?

---

### Chapter 7: Management Practices for Software
* **Summary:** Examines Lean management capabilities. It shows that external Change Advisory Boards (CABs) are negatively correlated with delivery performance and do not improve stability. Instead, organizations should use lightweight peer reviews, implement Work-in-Progress (WIP) limits, visualize work (Kanban), and use operational telemetry to make business decisions.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Change Approval Overhead:** Do we require external approvals (managers, CABs) for standard production changes? If yes, how can we transition to peer review (pull requests) and automated pipelines?
  * [ ] **WIP Limits:** Do we actively enforce WIP limits, or is the team context-switching between too many parallel tasks?
  * [ ] **Data-Driven Operations:** Do we use our system monitoring and application telemetry to make daily operational decisions and prioritize engineering work, or is monitoring only used to trigger alerts when things break?

---

### Chapter 8: Product Development
* **Summary:** Proves that Lean product development practices drive software delivery performance and generative culture. Key capabilities include working in small batches (slicing features so they take less than a week), understanding the value stream, gathering and implementing customer feedback, and team experimentation (allowing teams to change requirements based on user feedback).
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Batch Size:** Are our user stories decomposed so they can be completed, tested, and shipped in less than a week, or are we building large, multi-week feature batches?
  * [ ] **Team Experimentation:** Does our team have the autonomy to modify requirements or experiment with product design based on direct customer feedback, or are we strictly executing a top-down roadmap?
  * [ ] **Customer Feedback Loops:** How quickly does user feedback reach the development team, and is it incorporated into the backlog systematically?

---

### Chapter 9: Making Work Sustainable
* **Summary:** Discusses burnout (physical, mental, or emotional exhaustion) and how to prevent it. Burnout is strongly correlated with pathological cultures and deployment pain. The research shows that investments in technical CD capabilities and Lean management directly decrease burnout and make work sustainable.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Burnout Risk Indicators:** Are team members showing signs of burnout (exhaustion, cynicism/indifference about work, feeling ineffective)?
  * [ ] **Work Environment Risk Factors:** Assess the team's environment against the six risk factors for burnout:
    * *Work overload:* Is the team chronically understaffed or overloaded?
    * *Lack of control:* Do developers feel they have no say in how they work?
    * *Insufficient rewards:* Are successes celebrated, or are failures the only things noticed?
    * *Breakdown of community:* Is there collaboration or siloed isolation?
    * *Absence of fairness:* Are promotions, assignments, and credit distributed transparently?
    * *Value mismatch:* Do our actual operational practices match our stated company values?

---

### Chapter 10: Employee Satisfaction, Identity, and Engagement
* **Summary:** Proves that employee satisfaction and organizational identity (how much employees identify with and advocate for their company) are driven by good technical and Lean practices. High satisfaction is measured using employee Net Promoter Score (eNPS). Engaged, satisfied employees drive better business performance.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Tooling Quality:** Are we providing our team with modern, efficient tools and resources to do their job, or are they frustrated by slow hardware, lagging networks, and legacy software?
  * [ ] **eNPS Pulse:** Do our developers act as advocates for our team and company (would they recommend working here to a peer)?
  * [ ] **Skill Alignment:** Are team members using their skills and abilities effectively, or is their time spent on administrative tasks and manual toil?

---

### Chapter 11: Leaders and Managers
* **Summary:** Examines transformational leadership. Successful leaders show vision, inspirational communication, intellectual stimulation, supportive leadership, and personal recognition. Transformational leadership does not directly improve delivery performance, but it *predicts and enables* the technical, process, and cultural capabilities that do.
* **Engineering Manager Investigation Checklist:**
  * [ ] **Intellectual Stimulation:** Do I encourage my team to challenge status-quo assumptions and rethink old ways of solving problems, or do I enforce traditional compliance?
  * [ ] **Personal Recognition:** Do I systematically recognize and celebrate individual and team achievements (especially when paying down technical debt or improving capabilities), or are only major feature launches celebrated?
  * [ ] **Clear Vision:** Does the team understand *why* their current work matters to the broader strategic goals of the business?
