What this role actually is

One-line summary: You are TSO's programme delivery arm. You find where the CTO org is constrained, design programmes to fix it, and drive outcomes through influence rather than authority.

The first programme: Project Bloom

Embed EM accountability for Core4 improvement (Speed, Effectiveness, Quality, Impact) across the CTO org. Must reach outcome validation by December 2026. This is already underway — you inherit and own it.

  • Someone who can read DX/org data and turn it into a programme brief — not just a report
  • Credibility with senior engineers and EMs; cannot rely on title
  • Generative, not facilitative — you identify the problem AND build the solution
  • Clear writer: executive summaries AND engineering depth

Using data for org insights

What they want to see: You move from signal to diagnosis to programme — not from data to dashboard. The question is always "what does this tell us about where the org is stuck?"

The signal-to-programme model (build this narrative)

The role description says: "build a repeatable model for moving from DX signal to programme brief." Have a clear mental model you can describe in 60 seconds.

  1. Collect signals — DX survey results, DORA metrics, incident patterns, EM sentiment, retro themes
  2. Cluster and weight — which signals are consistent across teams? Which are outliers? What is the delta from last quarter?
  3. Diagnose the constraint type — is it structure, individual behaviour, group dynamics, goal clarity, or tooling? (Use the job description's own language here)
  4. Scope the programme response — what change, owned by whom, by when, measured how?
  5. Validate — run the programme, track leading indicators, close the loop

Diagnosis: finding the real constraint

Key idea: The job description distinguishes organisational friction from technical debt as a binding constraint. Interviewers will probe whether you can make that call — and whether you have made it before.

The core diagnostic question

"Is the team slow/ineffective because of technical debt (the code is hard to change), or because of organisational friction (the way people work together is the bottleneck)?"

Both are real. The role's job is the second. But you need credibility to make that diagnosis — which means understanding the first.

Diagnostic signals — organisational friction vs. technical constraint

SignalPoints to org frictionPoints to technical constraint
Deploy frequency is low Process gates, review bottlenecks, unclear ownership Long build/test cycle, fragile release pipeline
High incident rate No shared definition of done, QA not integrated, unclear accountability Architectural instability, missing test coverage
DX score low on "I can do my best work" Context switching, meeting load, unclear priorities Slow tooling, poor local dev experience
Core4 Impact score low Work not connected to player/business outcome, misaligned roadmap Technical platform doesn't support needed features

Diagnosis method to describe in interview

  1. Start with the metric that is worst. Ask: is this consistent across teams, or one outlier?
  2. Talk to 3–4 EMs. Not to validate the metric — to understand their mental model of why.
  3. Look for the gap between what people say in retros vs. what the data shows. That gap is often the real constraint.
  4. Ask: if we gave this team 20% more engineers, would this number improve? If yes, it is capacity. If no, it is process or org.
  5. Form a hypothesis. Test it cheaply before running a full programme.

Your SDK team context

You have shipped to 5 platforms from a single C++ codebase. You know what cross-team friction costs: broken platform assumptions, misaligned release schedules, unclear API contracts, game teams blocked by SDK decisions. That is first-hand diagnosis experience.

Technical credibility

What they need: "Sufficient technical credibility to be taken seriously in engineering conversations." You don't need to code — you need to ask the right questions and not be fooled by surface explanations.

What technical credibility looks like in this role

  • You can read a DORA dashboard and ask the right follow-up question (e.g. "what is your average PR size?" when lead time is high)
  • You know the difference between build time, test time, and review time — and which is the bottleneck
  • You can hold a conversation about platform boundaries, SDK versioning, and why a "simple fix" takes three weeks
  • You don't accept "it's complicated" — you ask for the specific complication
  • You have shipped software. Engineers will respect that history.

Building influence without authority

This is the hardest part of the role. You drive outcomes with senior EMs and craft leads who have no reporting line to you. The job description says: "drives outcomes through commitment and credibility."

The influence model — three levers

1. Credibility

You know what you are talking about. You have done the work. Engineers and EMs take you seriously in a technical conversation. You do not need to be told the answer — you come with a diagnosis.

2. Evidence

You use data to name the problem specifically. "Your lead time is 3x the org median and has been for two quarters" is harder to dismiss than "I think there might be a flow problem."

3. Connection to what they care about

EMs care about their team's output and their own credibility with leadership. Frame the programme in terms of what it does for them and their team — not for TSO's reporting.

4. Honest feedback loops

The job description explicitly names this. People engage when they trust you will hear bad news and not punish them for it. Build that reputation early.

Influencing senior leadership (CTO org)

  • Lead with the headline: what is the problem, how big is it, what does fixing it unlock
  • Quantify the cost of inaction: if this constraint stays, what does it cost over 6 months?
  • Give them a decision, not a discussion: bring options with a recommendation, not an open question
  • Track and report progress visibly: monthly check-in with a one-pager. No surprises.
  • Name the risks early: senior leaders trust people who flag problems before they escalate

Influencing EMs and craft leads (no formal authority)

  • Start with listening, not prescribing — they know their team, you know the org pattern
  • Show the data specific to their team, then the org comparison — most EMs respond to peer comparison
  • Make it easy to participate: lightweight asks, clear what is expected, visible when they act
  • Give them the win: when a team improves, make sure their EM is visible as the driver of that improvement
  • Distinguish resistance from disagreement — resistance is about trust, disagreement is about the approach. Treat them differently.

Prepare a story for each of these

  • A time you changed someone's mind (senior) with data and argument, not authority
  • A time someone resisted and you got them on board — what was the real barrier?
  • A time you had to escalate because influence wasn't enough — and how you handled it
  • A time you had to be honest with a leader about something they didn't want to hear
