1. Aspects to consider when forming a new team

  • Size: 5–9 people, ceiling ~15. Grounded in Dunbar's number — trust and decision quality degrade past this range. Amazon's "two-pizza team" is the practical version.
  • Team is the unit of delivery, not the individual — work should be assigned to teams, mixing generalists with a few specialists.
  • Design for cognitive load — a team's domain should fit inside what it can actually hold in its head.
  • Pick the right team type: stream-aligned (majority), platform, enabling, complicated-subsystem.
  • Conway's Law is not optional — team boundaries become system architecture boundaries; design them together.
  • Stability over ad-hoc project staffing — long-lived teams aligned to a stream build trust and context.
  • Humility, respect, trust — Google's "three pillars"; most team dysfunction traces back to a deficit in one of these.
  • Leaders should trust and get out of the way — direction and unblocking, not control.

Sources: Team Topologies (team size, cognitive load, team types, Conway's Law, stability) · Software Engineering at Google (three pillars, leadership trust)

2. Forming a team from well-established, long-tenured groups

  • Brooks's Law and re-"storming" — mixing people from different established teams can reduce capacity at first, not add it; expect a real dip before things gel.
  • You're breaking existing cohesion, not just building new cohesion.
    "Disbanding high-performing teams is worse than vandalism: it is corporate psychopathy." — Allan Kelly, quoted in Team Topologies
  • Tacit/tribal knowledge doesn't move automatically — plan deliberate knowledge-transfer time.
  • Existing habits and norms will collide — decide new team norms deliberately rather than letting them be fought out.
  • Team ownership and continuity of care — avoid splitting ownership of the same system across teams.
  • Use a "fracture plane" to cut system boundaries cleanly (business domain, compliance, data ownership) so the new team gets something it can own end-to-end.
  • Reorg motive matters — reorgs for management convenience or headcount optics actively damage delivery capability, and people can tell.
  • Rebuild trust deliberately — trust doesn't transfer automatically between people from different source teams.
  • Team size discipline still applies — resist merging everyone into one oversized team to avoid political pain.
  • Stability going forward — treat the new team as long-lived once formed.

Source: Team Topologies (Brooks's Law, Tuckman/storming, fracture planes, reorg motive, team ownership)

3. Positive aspects for the org of forming new teams

  • Shape architecture deliberately via the reverse Conway maneuver — form teams around the target architecture instead of getting it by accident.
  • Relieves overloaded teams — restores cognitive-load headroom (OutSystems Engineering Productivity example).
  • Restores intrinsic motivation — autonomy, mastery, purpose (Dan Pink), which sprawling teams erode.
  • Breaks down functional silos and empowers teams closer to the work.
  • Enables independent, parallel flow of work across the org.
  • Lets deep expertise scale without becoming a bottleneck (platform / complicated-subsystem teams).
  • Creates a home for capability-building without permanently loading down delivery teams (enabling teams with an expiration date).
  • Gives people new challenges and growth without leaving the org.
  • Injects fresh thinking into domains that have ossified inside long-established groups.
  • Improves accountability and ownership clarity — a crisp charter beats sprawling shared ownership.

Sources: Team Topologies (reverse Conway, cognitive load, silos, ownership) · Accelerate (architectural decoupling research, quoted within Team Topologies)

4. Why "start with why" matters when reorging

  • Purpose is a core intrinsic motivator — a reorg without a stated why creates a purpose vacuum that anxiety and rumor fill instead.
  • People can tell a real "why" from a fake one — reorgs driven by management convenience or headcount, not a genuine flow/architecture problem, are named as actively destructive, and the deception erodes trust.
  • Mandates without buy-in fail.
    "Trying to fix engineering issues by mandating them from above is doomed to failure — you really need buy-in from the folks working at the coalface." — Team Topologies case study
  • Clarity of purpose reduces friction cost of new team structures and interactions.
  • The why has to be the architecture/flow problem, not the org chart — otherwise Conway's Law pulls the structure back to old habits.
  • You're asking people to pay a real cost (productivity dip, broken trust) — they need to understand why it's worth it.

Sources: Team Topologies (Dan Pink's purpose, reorg motive, team charter case study, Conway's Law) · Accelerate (burnout & sense-of-purpose findings)

5. Involving current teams & team members in a reorg

Yes — the material treats this as essential, not optional:

  • Ask teams directly about their own cognitive load — a concrete, non-judgmental question that should drive where new boundaries get drawn.
  • Run an open workshop before mandating anything — publish a team charter and invite representatives from every affected team.
  • Staff the new team partly from people who know the problem — carry over developers from the existing teams, don't impose entirely from outside.
  • Broadcast the work, don't gatekeep it — ongoing visibility (demos, mob sessions, showcases) sustains buy-in beyond the kickoff.
  • Let teams weigh in on how interactions will actually work — collaboration vs. service vs. facilitation should be a live conversation, not a decree.
  • Both directions are needed — bottom-up enthusiasm alone stalls without leadership backing; top-down mandate alone fails without frontline buy-in.
  • Watch for false involvement: ad-hoc reactive team creation and repeated shuffling of team members without real input are named anti-patterns, not genuine participation.

Source: Team Topologies (cognitive-load check-ins, team charter case study, anti-patterns of ad hoc design and shuffling)

6. When reorg is actually the right move

The trap to name first: reorgs driven by "management convenience or reducing headcount" are explicitly called out as one of the most destructive patterns in software orgs. Legitimate circumstances instead:

  • A team's cognitive load has measurably exceeded capacity (via direct team check-in, not a hunch).
  • Architecture and team structure have drifted apart (Conway's Law mismatch) — the reverse Conway maneuver.
  • A collaboration mode has outlived its purpose — ongoing collaboration without a live discovery/capability-building reason is expensive and can mask deficiencies.
  • Interaction friction reveals a boundary problem — e.g. a team burning hours to consume something that should be a clean "service."
  • The organization has grown past a Dunbar threshold (~9, ~15, ~50, ~150).
  • A capability gap needs closing, via a team with an explicit mission and expiration date — not a permanent new silo.
  • Discovery is turning into steady-state delivery (or vice versa) — team interaction mode should evolve with the phase.

The test:

"Given our skills, constraints, cultural and engineering maturity, desired software architecture, and business goals, which team topology will help us deliver results faster and safer?" — Team Topologies
If that can't be answered concretely, the reorg isn't justified yet.

Source: Team Topologies (organizational sensing, team interaction modes, Dunbar's number, reverse Conway maneuver)
